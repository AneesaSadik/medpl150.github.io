Reflection One

Photography is something I use almost every day, but I rarely think about what it means to actually take a picture. I often take pictures of anything that interests me or is appealing, like nice scenery, my friends and family, or a plate of delicious food. After reading Susan Sontag’s “In Plato’s Cave,” however, photography seems much deeper than I initially believed. Susan Sontag presents the camera as something that can actually separate and divide us from further reality rather than helping us capture it. Her comparison between taking a photograph and using a weapon especially made me think about how much control the photographer has over the subject. When someone takes a picture, they have a uniquely important control over what someone who views the photograph gets to see and how they will remember and experience a specific moment. The photographer can leave out or include things that can impact the viewer’s ideas of the specific event. The viewer is not present during the moment and this can influence their view on it. This is especially relevant today because almost everyone carries a camera in their pocket. At concerts, vacations, restaurants, sporting events, and even ordinary moments with friends, it is normal to immediately take out a phone and take a picture. Sometimes it feels like we are documenting our lives while we are living them, rather than simply living them. The author also makes a point about how photographs can make real events feel less real. A photograph gives us the feeling that we have witnessed something, even when we have only seen an image of it. This becomes complicated when we see photographs of tragedies or suffering. Seeing hundreds of disturbing images online can make something that should feel shocking seem ordinary. This is heightened with social media, where someone can scroll for hours and experience many different images or videos that generate contrasting emotions that a human was not meant to experience rapidly. The more images we consume, the easier it can become to scroll past them and move on to the next thing. The photograph can bring an event to our attention, but it can also create a distance between us and actuality behind the image. If I were trying to represent our current moment through photography, I would focus on that idea of distance. I could photograph a person sitting alone while looking at their phone or a device, surrounded by other people who are also looking at screens. The image could show how connected we appear to be while also highlighting how physically separated we are from what is happening around us. This photograph would represent the contradiction of modern photography, and how we think that we are constantly capturing and viewing the world through the images we take, yet those images may be creating more distance between us and the actual reality that they represent. Susan Sontag’s argument makes me question whether taking a picture truly helps us experience a moment, or whether it sometimes prevents us from experiencing it fully.

Reflection Three

Artificial intelligence and robotic models that claim to answer your every question have become very prominent in the world, and specifically in education. AI like ChatGPT, especially, can have a diverse array of roles such as making certain tasks faster or, as some may prematurely think, easier. Research, however, raises an important question about such models. If technology does the work for us, are we actually learning? I think this question is particularly important after reading “Your Brain on ChatGPT,” and watching “Brain Experts WARNING: Watch This Before Using ChatGPT Again! (Shocking New Discovery).” Though researchers described their findings as preliminary, their conclusions were substantial. One study, for instance, reported that people who wrote essays using ChatGPT claimed ownership of their work less and had a much harder time remembering what they actually wrote compared to people who wrote the same essays without outside information or help. Although those who wrote their essays with AI took less time, they did not actually learn about the material to a proficient degree to even remember what they wrote in their essay. One of the biggest takeaways from this study is that education should go deeper than just doing assignments for the sake of a grade or because it is the task at hand, but rather to learn the material and actually deepen your knowledge and understanding. Using AI to do so often takes away from the latter in favor of the former, which goes back to the original question. If a person can produce a well written essay without fully understanding the ideas behind it, then the finished assignment may look successful while the actual learning and comprehension of the material is limited. In this sense, the time saved by AI comes at the cost of developing the very skills that were supposed to come from the assignment in the first place, and even broader skills such as critical thinking, memory, writing, and problem solving. At the same time, someone could argue that AI is not the problem, but rather how people intend to use it. AI can theoretically be used as a tool without replacing the learning process, though cautiously. For example, a person using AI to create an entire essay compared to a person using AI to explain a difficult concept, provide another perspective of their work, or help identify what needs improving are quite different uses. In the latter, the student is still responsible for their work and their understanding of the material, and in fact may even have a deeper knowledge of the material compared to the prior example. This makes me think that the real issue may not be whether AI belongs in education, but where the line should be drawn between assistance and replacement of a person’s own thinking. Technology has been introduced into all areas of life to make certain tasks more efficient, so completely rejecting new iterations of it may not be realistic. However, efficiency should not become the main goal if it comes at the expense of learning. In the end, although time saving, AI should not be used for education to merely replace the learning process. Thinking on your own, and using your brain is way more valuable than sacrificing your knowledge in favor or false efficiency, of which, if used correctly, AI can help rather than hinder.
